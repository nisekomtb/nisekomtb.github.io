# Tom's voice (first-person stories)

Governs `categories: stories` posts written in Tom's voice. Nothing else.

`.claude/rules/copy.md` remains correct for house voice on Twin Peaks pages, partner pages,
event listings and the shop. Two voices coexist deliberately, and the story voice is looser
than the house one.

---

## Do not use the site as a source for this document

**Published pages on namba.ngo are not a voice source.** They were assembled from multiple
inputs, then run through an SEO pass and the humanizer skill. The result is a real and

Section 7 covers what changes on the way to prose.

## 1. How the sentences are actually built

**Additive, not subordinated.** Clauses chain with `and`, `and then`, `so`. He builds
sequences, rarely nests them.

> "So we moved everything over. We moved all our kite surfing gear and our mountain bikes and
> our life back to Niseko. And then we arrived in April."

**Long sentences are lists of concrete steps, not elaborate constructions.** When explaining
the permit process he runs eight clauses in a row and every one of them is a physical action:
flag a route, brush cut it, submit the maps, wait, revise, wait, build, rewild.

**Repetition carries emphasis.** He does not reach for a stronger adjective, he says the same
word more times.

> "lap after lap after lap, and it gets faster and faster and faster"
> "you just eat it and eat it and eat it and eat it until it's out of season"
> "loved it, loved the atmosphere, loved the prizes, loved the vibes"

Keep this. It is the single most recognisable thing he does, and cleaning it into "lap after
lap, getting steadily faster" destroys the voice on contact.

**Rhetorical question, then a short flat answer.**

> "But what do I do in a week? What do I do in a week? Good question. A lot."

## 2. The actual vocabulary

Enthusiasm is plain and repeated, never literary. The full observed set: **really cool,
amazing, awesome, incredible, crazy, insane, great, good, stoked, beautiful, lovely**. That
is the whole range. He does not reach past it, and neither should a draft.

`just` as an intensifier, constantly: "just really cool", "just so high", "they were just
fearless", "we're just putting it all back into the dirt".

British, and it should stay British: **gone mental**, **dog's body**, **go see a man about a
dog**, **janky**, **chuffed** territory generally. Riding vocabulary is MTB-standard:
**sending it**, **whip**, **party train**, **lapping**, **berms**, **side hits**.

Negatives are understated to the point of dry. This is a real register and worth protecting:

> "a bit of a disappointment"
> "not really up to par"
> "some of the sasa didn't get the message"
> "They have a slightly different trajectory"

## 3. Hedging and self-undercutting

Constant, and it is not weakness, it is how he sounds honest. `sort of`, `a bit`, `probably`,
`maybe`, `I think`, `I suppose`, `I mean`.

He actively refuses credit and defers to whoever actually did the work:

> "I don't actually do any of the trail building side of things... that's not really a
> question for me, that one."
> "these are all the questions for Dan and Angharad, really"
> "It might have been Dan's idea, actually. I don't want to completely credit him."

He flags his own uncertainty unprompted:

> "I'm not really sure if this is true or not, so you need to probably look this up"

**A draft that states things more confidently than he did is wrong**, even when the confident
version is more readable. If he hedged it, hedge it.

The hedging survives even when he is annoyed, which is a specific and useful finding: see §10.

## 4. Openers and closers

Opens with `So`, `I mean`, `Yeah, I mean`, or by repeating a fragment of the question:
"A bad day at Twin Peaks. I mean, yeah..."

Closes by trailing off, not by summarising: `so yeah`, `that sort of stuff`, `and that's it`,
`yeah?`. He never writes a concluding sentence that restates the section. **Do not add one.**
An AI-drafted section reliably ends with a tidy summarising line and that is the loudest tell
in the whole draft.

`that sort of stuff` and `and stuff` appear well over thirty times. In prose most have to go
(section 7), but keeping one or two per piece is right.

## 5. What he never does

- No grand claims. Asked to sell Niseko in summer he reaches for asparagus, blueberries,
  onsen, matsuri fireworks. Concrete nouns, not adjectives.
- No romanticising. Asked for the best run of his life he answers with a route by trail name
  and a physical sensation. No transcendence, no metaphor.
- No "journey", "passion", "dedicated", "commitment", "vibrant", "boasts", "nestled".
- No balanced triads. He lists four or six things, or two, as it happens to fall.
- No section-closing summary. See section 4.
- No em dashes, per house rule and matching how he actually builds sentences anyway.

## 6. Lines worth keeping verbatim

The best material in the transcript is unimprovable and no model would generate it. When one
of these fits, use it exactly:

> "Some of the sasa didn't get the message."
> "You can see we're just putting it all back into the dirt."
> "Relax more, you go faster. It doesn't seem to make any sense, but that seems to be what happens."
> "We're not here to make a profit, we're here to make assets."
> "Every mountain bike association should have their own beer."
> "The realisation that nobody would do it. We would have to do it ourselves."
> "He was just having the time of his life."
> "Now there are trail building jobs in Japan." (Cyril, ex-trail builder, quoted by Tom)

**Hunt for these in every transcript.** One genuinely good phrase kept intact is worth more
than a page of well-turned prose, and they are always hiding in the middle of a rambling
answer rather than at the start of a crisp one.

## 7. Speech into prose: what to cut, what to keep

He does not write how he talks, so a straight transcript is not the draft. But the edits are
narrower than instinct suggests.

**Cut:** `you know` (pure filler, ~every third sentence), `obviously` where it marks discourse
rather than meaning, false starts and restarts, most instances of `that sort of stuff`,
duplicated words from self-correction.

**Keep:** the additive `and then` chains, the repetition-for-emphasis, the hedges, the plain
enthusiasm words, the British idiom, the deferring of credit, the concrete nouns and names and
numbers, the trailing close.

**Never do:** promote a hedged statement to a confident one; replace a repeated word with a
smarter synonym; add a summarising sentence; upgrade "really cool" to something more
evocative; convert a chain of `and` into a subordinate clause because it reads tighter. Each
of those is individually defensible and collectively fatal.

The test at the end is section 9.

## 8. How this document gets updated

**After every story, diff Tom's edits against the draft and write the rules that fall out.**
That diff is the only direct measurement of the gap between what a model produces and what he
will put his name on. Nothing else measures it.

Log each round below, so the reasoning behind a rule survives.

### Round 1 — 2026-08-19, Whip Off draft, humanizer pass

**CONTRACTIONS. This is the finding.** The first draft ran 21 expanded forms ("do not", "it
is", "that is") against 5 contractions. Measured against the interview transcript, Tom speaks
at **543 contractions to 19 expanded, a ratio of about 29 to 1**. The site's own event copy
uses them freely too. So the draft was dramatically more formal than either Tom or the house
voice, and nothing in sections 1 to 10 caught it.

**Write in contractions by default.** Expand only for deliberate emphasis, and treat any draft
with more than a couple of expanded forms as wrong. The exception is "there are", since
"there're" is not written English.

Why it happened, so it does not happen again: avoiding em dashes and keeping the register tidy
pulled the whole piece formal as a side effect. Over-formalisation is a standard LLM tell and
it hides from a tells checklist, because every individual sentence is clean.

**A de-contracted verbatim quote is the worst version of this.** The pull quote went out as
"you know you have got the right speed" when he said "you've got". If a quote is marked
verbatim, diff it against the transcript character by character.

Also cut in this pass, both mine rather than his:

- "No clock, no gates, no race." A rhetorical triad of negations, copywriter cadence. Replaced
  with a plain statement.
- "which tells you something about who this is for", an editorial gloss on the age spread. He
  states concrete facts and lets them land; he does not append the moral. Deleted, the numbers
  kept.

Two triads survived the pass and were correct to keep: "style, height and execution" is the
actual scoring criteria (recorded as "intensity" until Tom corrected it on 2026-08-24) and "Mark, Andy and Meg" is three real people. Rule-of-three is a tell
when ideas are *forced* into threes, not when a real list happens to have three items.

### Round 2 — 2026-08-19, Tom's review of the Whip Off draft

**Do not draw attention to what a photograph or a story is missing.** Tom's note, on a caption
reading "The rider briefing. Not everybody who entered is in this one.": that is a strange
thing to point at. Nobody reading it would have wondered. Seven instances, and the pattern is
consistent enough to be a rule:

| Written | Problem | Now |
|---|---|---|
| "Not everybody who entered is in this one." | Flags an absence nobody noticed | "before the first warm-up laps" |
| "The women's field, all of it" | Makes a three-rider field sound like a shortfall | Names the three riders |
| "which isn't a whip and was never going to score" | Knocks the run it is illustrating | "the crowd loved him for it" |
| "Three people enjoying themselves between runs" | Admits in public that I could not identify them | "A good afternoon at the side of the trail" |
| "Three riders entered, which was the entire field" | Same shortfall framing as above | Names them and moves on |
| "the best thing on the prize table and cost almost nothing" | Undersells Dan's prize | Cut the cost clause |
| "That isn't the pitch I expected to be making" | Undercuts the pitch inside the sentence making it | Cut |

**Where this comes from.** All seven were mine, not lifted from a transcript, and six of the
seven were in captions. Captions are where an author starts talking about the material instead
of about the day, and that is where the hedging creeps in.

**The distinction that matters.** This is NOT a licence to remove his own understatement. He
says "a bit of a disappointment" and "they don't look so polished" and those stay, because they
are his voice and they are affectionate. What goes is the *author* apologising for the
coverage, doubting the subject, or flagging a gap. His hedges are about the world; these were
about the article.

Four negative-sounding lines were checked and deliberately kept: "there's no clock and it isn't
a race" (that is the format), "than run a purist whip-off category with nobody in it" (justifies
a good decision), "watching costs you nothing at all and asks nothing of you" (the no-barrier
point), and "Launch Control with nobody on it, which doesn't happen often on a Saturday" (which
implies the place is busy, so it is a compliment).

### Round 6 — 2026-08-24, Tom on the Whip Off: cut the explaining, keep the day

Round 5's diagnosis was warmth. Round 6 is the other half of the same problem and it names the
cause: **"reduce the descriptive text... rather than who is Peaty's, what are the exact layout
of the jump line and what happened at every second of the event."**

Three named offenders, and they are all the same move. Each was a passage where the draft
stopped telling the story and started explaining a thing:

| Passage | Was | Now |
|---|---|---|
| Who Peaty's are | Its own 46-word paragraph, mascot and UK events | One clause in the registration paragraph: they put up the prizes |
| Launch Control's layout | 228 words, hub routing, jump-by-jump order, both exits | 117 words: the climb, the Yotei view, the pull quote, and lapping it |
| The competition format | Warm-up laps, three scored runs, judges deliberating, repeated per category | Stated once, in chapter 01, where it belongs |

The two explaining chapters went 402 words to 220, a 45% cut, and nothing factual was lost
that a reader needs. **Explanation compounds silently.** No single sentence in either chapter
was wrong or padded, which is exactly why it survived five rounds. It only reads as dry in
aggregate, so it has to be measured per chapter rather than per sentence.

**A recap is not a trail guide.** The jump line description was the best material in the second
recording and it went in on that basis. But routing and jump order belong on `/twin-peaks/`,
where someone is deciding what to ride. In a recap they are the reader's homework before they
are allowed to enjoy the day. Keep the *sensation* of the trail (the climb, the view, lapping
it and lapping it) and drop the *specification*.

**Sponsor context is a clause, not a paragraph.** Peaty's are named in the title, the seoTitle,
the prize photo alt text and the medals. A paragraph explaining who they are on top of that was
serving the sponsor, not the reader.

**What replaced the cut words, per Round 5's four mechanisms:** the lede now says the day was
one of the best we have had up there, the crowd stayed on the bank "the whole afternoon", the
lapping repeats four times instead of three, the party train gets an exclamation mark, and
chapter 04 closes on "Watch these three."

**Two of his own Round 4 lines were in the way, and one did not survive.** "this is what you're
in for" was kept and moved to the head of the shortened chapter 02. "We're not over yet!" could
not be: it introduced the post-big-jump corners, which is the enumeration the round exists to
cut. Its exclamation mark was re-sited on the party train rather than lost. **When a cut lands on
a line traceable to his own rewrite, say so and account for it.** Those lines are the highest
grade material in the file.

**Two additions need Tom's sign-off**, recorded here because they are opinion rather than fact
and this document exists to stop me inventing his: "Best two minutes of the whole day!" on the
party train, and "one of the best days we've had up there" in the lede. Both are the kind of
thing he says and neither is in a recording. If either is wrong, that is a finding, not a typo.

**Still open, unchanged from Round 5:** the results section is three podium cards and no story.
How close the men's was, what surprised him, what a properly flat one sounded like from the
bank. Round 6 made the piece warmer everywhere except there, because there is nothing to be
warm with.

## 9. The read-aloud gate

Tom reads the draft out loud before it ships. Anything he stumbles over gets rewritten.

This is not a nicety. His mouth refuses sentences he would not say, and it catches failures
that no checklist does, particularly sentences that are grammatical and idiomatic and simply
not his.

## 10. The critical register

Added after `going-wrong.m4a`, 2026-08-18. Sections 1 to 9 were built from an interview in
which Tom skipped almost every question inviting criticism, so they describe enthusiastic and
explanatory Tom only. This section covers annoyed Tom, and the differences are smaller and
more specific than expected.

**The hedges do not go away.** Even at his most irritated he is still saying "I'm not exactly
sure why" and "a bit really". Annoyance does not make him more certain, which is the opposite
of what a model reaching for a "frustrated" register will produce.

**Understatement is the whole mechanism.** The scale runs from "a bit of a pain" through "just
such a waste of money" to the ceiling:

> "They were just starting to take the piss a bit really."

That is peak annoyance in nine months of material. **A draft that goes past it is wrong.** No
outrage, no rhetorical questions, no "frankly". If something warrants more than "taking the
piss", he still says "taking the piss".

**`just` does more work here than anywhere else.** "They were just trying to rip us off",
"it's just such a waste of money", "it was just, they were just starting to take the piss".
It is carrying the emotion that a more heated writer would put in the verb.

**Criticism arrives as flat parallel structure, not adjectives.** The sharpest line in the
recording contains no judging words at all:

> "They either just don't do it when they say they've done it, or they say they've done it
> when they haven't done it."

That construction, two mirrored clauses stating behaviour and letting the reader draw the
conclusion, is how he lands a real criticism. Preserve it exactly when it appears. Rewriting
it as "they were unreliable about permits" deletes the entire effect.

**He self-interrupts more when recalling something that annoyed him**, and the recall is
visible: "Or in this case, they, um, in this case, they, what did they do?" Cut the stumble in
prose, but note it as a signal that what follows is the part he cares about.

**He resolves rather than leaving it sour.** Every critical passage ends constructively, and
this is consistent enough to treat as a rule. The connection story closes on the workaround
Hokkaido Forest suggested and how the resorts will be joined after all. **Do not end a
critical passage on the criticism.** He does not, and a story that does will not read as him.

One line from this recording worth keeping verbatim, for §6:

> "It's just slipping away a little bit further and further."

### Round 3 — 2026-08-24, Tom's review of the Whip Off results section

**"We also dont need the behind the scenes bit about setup."** The cut passage was the
volunteers gathering at half eight, and the tent, tables and prizes being carried up the
access trail. Written because it was in the interview and it was true.

The rule it gives: **a recap is written for someone who was there or who might come next
time, and neither of them needs the operations.** What survived the cut was the half of the
paragraph a future entrant can act on: registration ran from the tent, waivers were signed
in advance, turn up and get a plate on your bars, the trail was closed to everyone else.
Same subject, but one half is the organisers' day and the other half is the reader's.

The distinction is NOT "cut the unglamorous detail". Tom's own best material is
unglamorous: brake rotors and a wooden disc, "they don't look so polished". A detail earns
its place when the reader can feel it or use it. Carrying tables up a hill is neither.

Watch for this specifically after an interview, where the answer to "how did the day go"
naturally starts at the beginning of the organisers' day rather than the reader's.

### Round 4 — 2026-08-24, Tom's own rewrite of the Whip Off draft

The most useful round so far, because he did not give notes: he edited the file. That is the
diff this document was created to read (see the warning at the top about published copy not
being a voice source; THIS is the real thing).

**He cuts captions to labels.** Mine were sentences. His replacements: "The winners.",
"Whippet + Devo hat.", "Who doesn't love a free sticker?", "The party train, one lap for the
crowd!". Where I wrote "The winners, and then everybody rode down to the bottom together"
he wrote "The winners." Default to a label, not a sentence, and let the photograph carry it.

**He addresses the reader directly and uses exclamation marks.** "this is what you're in
for", "We're not over yet!". I had neither anywhere in the draft. His prose talks TO
someone; mine described something to nobody in particular.

**His intensifiers are "genuinely", "epic", "stoked".** "a genuinely epic view of Yotei",
"We were genuinely stoked". Not "remarkable", "striking", "superb".

**He deletes my explanations of his own decisions.** I wrote that the women's-category jump
choice "was a design decision rather than a concession"; he cut the defence and kept the
reason. Also gone: "There was a lot of woo-ing", "I don't think that's just a nice thing to
say" survived but the surrounding scaffolding did not. He does not argue with an objection
the reader has not made yet.

**He is warmer about people than I am.** "he was just having the time of his life", "which
he took very seriously", "Complete bragging rights". My versions of those same beats were
factual.

**One tic to normalise rather than preserve: he uses a semicolon where a colon belongs.**
"For the winners; bespoke medals", "The party train; one lap for the crowd". Three instances
this round. Fix them silently, it is punctuation rather than voice. Same for the handful of
missing apostrophes ("its" for "it's") and "usual" for "usually".

**What this changes for drafting:** write the caption as a label first and only expand it if
the label genuinely fails. Put a "you" in the prose. And do not pre-empt criticism of a
decision he is happy with.

### Round 5 — 2026-08-24, outside reader on the Whip Off: "dry, no flare"

Not Tom's edits this time, a first outside read. Verdict: "very matter of fact, very dry,
not very fun, no flare." Measured against the transcript, and the numbers found it.

| Marker | Draft | Tom |
|---|---|---|
| First person singular | 1 in 1,021 words | constant |
| Enthusiasm words | 5 | plain but everywhere |
| `just` as intensifier | 3 | "constantly", §2 |
| Hedges | 2 | "constant", §3 |
| Exclamation marks | 1 (his own, added in Round 4) | §Round 4 |

**Second-person address is not evenly spread, and that is the diagnosis.** All 19 instances
of "you" sat in the trail-description chapter and the closing invitation. The chapters
covering the actual day contained one "you", one "I" and no reaction to anything. The event
narrative was in reporting register while only the explaining and inviting chapters talked to
a human. Check the distribution per chapter, not the total.

**The confirming tell: every line a reader would call fun was a line Tom wrote himself** in
Round 4. Mine were the flat ones around them. If the lively sentences in a draft are all
traceable to his own rewrite, the draft has no voice of its own yet.

**Round 5 is caused partly by Rounds 2 and 3 being right.** Round 2 cut seven author-hedging
lines, Round 3 the setup passage, Round 4 the woo-ing. Each cut was correct. But colour came
out and nothing went back in, so the piece got tighter and cooler in the same pass. **After a
cutting round, check warmth, not just accuracy.**

**The unused-notes finding, which is the biggest one.** The story had used every *fact* in the
notes file and almost none of the *feeling*. Still sitting unused at Round 5: the crowd being
a surprise (fifty came purely to watch, against an expectation of volunteers plus a couple of
NAMBA people), riders going one at a time with about a minute between them, Tom's own
"they don't look so polished, a bit more funky" about the medals against the draft's word
"bespoke", music on at the tent, and the fact that Monty is his son. Mine the notes for
atmosphere on a second pass, separately from mining them for facts. The facts get used first
because they are easy to place.

**Flare does not come from adjectives.** His enthusiasm vocabulary is fixed and plain (§2), so
reaching for better adjectives to fix dryness is the §7 failure mode with extra steps. It
comes from four things: talking to the reader, repeating a word instead of upgrading it,
weirdly specific nouns, and open warmth about people.

**Two things deliberately NOT added**, recorded so the next pass does not re-litigate them:
Hamaya and Ogawa both riding yellow Santa Cruzes (true, and flat trivia with no payoff in
prose; it is a captioning aid, not a story beat), and the "fatties fit fine" sticker on
Stephanie's chainstay (funny in a bike shop, reads badly next to a named woman rider on a
public page).

**Still open, and it needs him:** the results section is three podium cards and no story. How
close the men's was, whether anything surprised him, what a properly flat one sounded like
from the bank. None of that is recorded anywhere, and inventing it is the one thing this
document exists to prevent.

### Round 6 — 2026-08-24, Tom's hard trim of the Whip Off, and the review of it

He rewrote the whole story himself and cut it from 1,100 words to 385, against 49
photographs. Two findings, both structural rather than sentence-level.

**AFTER A TRIM, RE-READ EVERY HEADING AGAINST THE BODY UNDER IT.** This is the finding.
Every problem the review turned up was a chapter title still promising something the
trimmed body no longer delivered:

| Heading | What the body had lost |
|---|---|
| "Come and watch next summer" | The walk-up-and-watch passage. Nothing left about how to come and watch |
| "Brake rotors and bragging rights" | Any mention of a brake rotor. Only the alt text still had it |
| "Peaty's supplying the good[s]" | Nothing, but it had gained the rider-briefing photo, which is not about prizes |
| "The kids were the best bit" | Still the best writing, now the shortest chapter on the page |

The trim itself was good. Cutting prose does not cut the headings, the photographs or the
front matter, so the piece silently develops promises it no longer keeps. Sentence-level
review does not catch this; reading the table of contents against the page does.

He resolved them by moving the body, not the heading, in three of the four cases: "before
the first run" absorbed the briefing photo, the medals line gained its brake rotor, and
the invitation stayed cut with the heading rewritten to "We'll be doing it again".

**THE SEMICOLON TIC IS SYSTEMATIC, NOT OCCASIONAL.** Round 4 logged three instances and
called it a tic. This round had six in 385 words, every one where a comma or a colon
belongs: "sideways; and a whip off", "Our guest judges; Mark", "natural habitat; he can be
found", "jobs of the day; sticker duty", "party train; styling things out", "jump line;
Launch Control". At that rate it is a reliable feature of his drafts. Fix them silently
on sight, as punctuation rather than voice, and do not raise them as questions.

**He wrote "testament" himself**, which §5 lists under "what he never does". That list was
built from a transcript, so it describes spoken Tom; this is the first evidence that
written Tom does reach for the register the list bans. He chose to reword rather than keep
it when asked, so the rule stands, but **treat §5 as a prompt to ask rather than a rule to
enforce when the word is in his own draft.**

**Numbers: he cut them, then put them back when the consequence was named.** The lede lost
"Thirteen riders, fifty spectators" while the meta description kept claiming 13 riders.
Pointing at the mismatch, rather than at the missing data, is what got them restored.
Front matter is part of the copy and does not get trimmed alongside the body.

One process note: the layout problems were all measurable. Chapter heights ran 12:1
between longest and shortest, and the longest chapter had absorbed four unrelated beats.
Measuring the rendered height of each section found in one pass what reading the markdown
did not.
